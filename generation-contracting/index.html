The property manager’s challenge

Damage affects more than one room or one work order

A major property issue can quickly become a coordination problem involving residents, tenants, owners, insurance representatives, building staff, and multiple contractors. A well-managed response helps reduce confusion and keeps the next decision clear.

01

Protect the property

The first priority is limiting additional damage and identifying areas that require immediate attention, documentation, or temporary protection.

02

Keep people informed

Residents, tenants, owners, and insurers need timely updates about what happened, what is being done, and what happens next.

03

Coordinate the work

A damaged property may require demolition, drying, cleaning, repairs, reconstruction, and specialty trades. Coordination matters at every stage.

04

Restore normal operations

The goal is a safe, complete repair that helps residents return, tenants reopen, and owners protect the value of the property.

Fire and smoke damage

After a fire, a property may require a combination of cleaning, demolition, odor-related work, structural repairs, finish replacement, and rebuilding.

A clear project plan helps property managers understand what can be restored, what must be replaced, and how the work will be coordinated.

Storm and exterior damage

Wind, hail, heavy rain, and other severe weather can affect roofs, siding, windows, interiors, and shared spaces.

Generation Contracting can help identify the visible and related repair needs so the property is not left with an incomplete fix.

Multi-unit restoration

Damage in an apartment or condominium building can affect several units, hallways, mechanical areas, or common spaces at once.

A unit-by-unit plan can help organize access, resident communication, repairs, and the sequence of work.

Commercial repairs

Offices, retail spaces, restaurants, warehouses, and other commercial properties may need repairs while operations continue in another area.

The scope should account for safety, access, customer or employee activity, and the business’s need to resume normal operations.

Insurance coordination

Property managers often serve as the communication link between owners, occupants, contractors, and insurance representatives.

Organized documentation and a clearly defined scope can make it easier for everyone to understand the work being proposed.

A practical response process

What property managers can expect

The process should give you a clear understanding of the property’s condition, the recommended work, and the decisions that need to be made.

1

Initial conversation and site review

Share what happened, who is affected, what has already been done, and what access or safety restrictions are in place. A site review helps establish the starting point.

2

Scope and priorities

Separate urgent protection and mitigation needs from the larger repair and reconstruction scope. Identify the areas that should be addressed first.

3

Communication and coordination

Establish who needs updates, how access will be managed, and how the work will be coordinated with owners, occupants, insurers, and building representatives.

4

Repair and reconstruction

Move through the approved work with attention to safety, quality, sequencing, site conditions, and the building’s operational needs.

5

Completion review

Review the completed work, identify any remaining items, and confirm that the repaired area is ready for the next stage of occupancy or operations.

Questions property managers often ask

What should I do first after property damage?

Start with safety and immediate protection. Document what you can, notify the appropriate owner or insurance contact, restrict unsafe areas, and contact a qualified contractor or restoration professional to help assess the situation.

Do not begin major demolition or discard potentially relevant materials without understanding the documentation and insurance requirements for the property.

The work plan should account for occupancy, access, noise, dust, safety, and communication. Discuss those requirements during the initial site review so the sequence of work can reflect the people using the building.
