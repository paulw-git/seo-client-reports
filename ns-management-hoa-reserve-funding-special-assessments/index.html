HOA Reserve Funding and Special Assessments

How association boards can balance today’s assessment burden with the long-term cost of maintaining shared property and major community assets.

Reserve funding is a long-term planning decision. The board must consider the condition of the community’s major assets, the timing of future projects, current homeowner assessments, and the financial effect of waiting too long to prepare.

Regular contributions

A planned portion of assessments builds reserves gradually over time.

Special assessments

A one-time charge may help fund an urgent or underfunded major project.

Project planning

A clear capital plan helps the board make decisions before a repair becomes an emergency.

How regular reserve contributions work

The most common reserve-funding approach is to include a planned contribution in the association’s annual budget. Homeowner assessments then support both recurring operating needs and the long-term reserve plan, with the amounts allocated according to the association’s budget and governing requirements.

Contributing consistently can make future projects easier to manage because the cost is spread across multiple budget cycles. The appropriate amount depends on the community’s assets, their condition, projected replacement costs, existing reserve balance, and the funding recommendations available to the board.

When a special assessment may be considered

A special assessment is an additional charge levied for a specific association need. It may be considered when a major project is more urgent than expected, when reserves are not sufficient, or when an unforeseen repair creates a significant financial obligation.

Special assessments can provide a way to address a real need, but they may also create financial pressure for homeowners and make budgeting more difficult. The board should document the reason for the assessment, explain the project and expected cost, and follow the association’s governing documents and applicable requirements.

The cost of waiting too long

Underfunding reserves does not eliminate the cost of future repairs. It may instead shift more of the cost into a shorter period, leaving the board with fewer options when a major component reaches the end of its useful life.

Waiting can also reduce the board’s ability to schedule work strategically. An emergency repair may limit contractor choices, compress the timeline for collecting funds, or require the association to consider financing and other measures that would have been less urgent with earlier planning.

Why operating and reserve money should stay distinct

Operating funds are generally intended for recurring services and current obligations. Reserve funds are intended for major, infrequent capital needs. Using one fund casually to cover the other can make the association’s financial position harder to understand and may conflict with governing documents or applicable requirements.

If a transfer or temporary exception is being considered, the board should evaluate the purpose, document the decision, and obtain appropriate accounting or legal guidance. The goal is to understand the effect on both current cash flow and future capital needs.

How boards can explain funding decisions

Homeowners are more likely to understand assessment changes when the board explains what the money supports and how the decision fits into the community’s longer-term plan. Clear communication can include the condition of the affected asset, the expected project timing, available reserves, the proposed funding method, and the consequences of delaying the work.
