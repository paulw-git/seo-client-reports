Condo Association Financial Statements Explained

What board members should review each month to understand the association’s financial position, activity, and cash flow.

A practical review order

Financial reports are management tools, not just paperwork. A clear monthly reporting package helps a board understand what the association owns, what it owes, what it collected, what it spent, and where attention may be needed next.

Board members do not need to be accountants to ask good financial questions. They do need a consistent process for reviewing the reports, comparing actual activity with the budget, and following up on unusual balances or transactions.

This guide introduces the main reports commonly included in an association’s monthly financial package. The exact format may vary by association, accounting system, governing documents, and professional guidance.

The monthly financial package at a glance

Balance sheet

A snapshot of assets, liabilities, and fund balances at a specific date.

Income statement

A summary of income and expenses for a selected period.

Cash disbursements

A record of payments made by the association.

General ledger

Detailed transaction activity organized by accounting code.

Homeowner activity

Balances and account activity for individual homeowners.

Treasurer’s report

A board-level summary that highlights key financial checks.

Balance sheet

A balance sheet shows the association’s financial position at a particular point in time. It generally groups information into assets, liabilities, and the association’s fund balance or equity.

Assets

Assets may include checking accounts, reserve accounts, investments, prepaid expenses, or money owed to the association. The board can compare current balances with prior months and ask whether large changes have a clear explanation.

Liabilities

Liabilities may include unpaid bills, vendor invoices, payroll-related obligations, loans, or other amounts the association owes. A growing payable balance may be normal during a billing cycle, but it should be understandable and monitored.

Fund balances

Fund balances help show the accumulated financial position of the association. Boards often review operating and reserve funds separately because they serve different purposes and may be governed by different budgets or restrictions.

Income statement

An income statement compares revenue and expenses over a defined period. It may show the current month, the year to date, the budget, and the difference between budgeted and actual amounts.

For income, the board may review assessments, late fees, interest, user fees, and other revenue. For expenses, the board may review utilities, insurance, management, repairs, maintenance, professional services, administrative costs, and other categories included in the association’s budget.

A variance is not automatically a problem. A repair may be higher than budget because a planned project was completed early, while an expense may be lower because an invoice has not arrived yet. The useful question is whether the difference is expected, explainable, and reflected in the association’s outlook.

Cash disbursements

A cash-disbursement report lists payments made during a period. It can help the board review vendors, amounts, payment dates, invoice descriptions, and the account or category charged.

Boards can use this report to confirm that major payments match approved work, contracts, budgets, or board decisions. The report is also a practical place to identify duplicate-looking entries, unfamiliar vendors, unusually large payments, or recurring charges that deserve a closer look.

General ledger

A general ledger provides transaction-level detail by accounting code. If an income-statement category looks unusually high, the ledger can help show which invoices or payments contributed to that total.

The ledger is most useful when a board starts with a specific question. For example: “Why are repairs over budget?” or “What payments make up this professional-services total?” Reviewing every transaction without a purpose can be time-consuming, while tracing a meaningful variance can produce a clearer answer.

Homeowner activity report

A homeowner activity report can show balances for individual owners and help the association monitor assessment activity. Depending on the system, it may include charges, payments, credits, late fees, and outstanding balances.

This information should be handled carefully because it may contain personal or account-specific data. Board members should follow the association’s privacy practices and use the report for legitimate association business.

Treasurer’s report

A treasurer’s report gives the board a concise way to review key financial information during a meeting. It may summarize bank balances, budget performance, outstanding items, and account reconciliations.

Bank reconciliationsHave the association’s bank records been reconciled, and are unexplained differences being followed up?
Bank statementsAre statements available for the accounts the board is reviewing?
Budget performanceWhich income or expense categories are materially different from budget?
Outstanding itemsAre there unpaid assessments, vendor bills, checks, or deposits that need attention?
