Operating vs. Reserve Funds

Understanding the two financial pools that help an HOA or condo association manage today’s needs while preparing for tomorrow’s major repairs.

Operating fund

Pays for recurring expenses and the daily work required to keep the community running.

Reserve fund

Sets aside money for major repairs, replacements, and long-term capital needs.

Reserve study

Helps estimate useful lives, future costs, and a reasonable long-term funding plan.

The central distinction: Operating funds support the association’s current obligations. Reserve funds help prepare for large, infrequent expenses. Keeping the purposes clear gives the board a better view of cash flow, risk, and future needs.

Both funds are important, but they should not be treated as interchangeable. A healthy association needs enough operating cash to pay its regular bills and a realistic reserve strategy for the major components that will eventually need repair or replacement.

Operating funds: supporting daily association life

The operating fund is the association’s day-to-day financial engine. It is usually supported by regular homeowner assessments and is used for predictable, recurring, or smaller unexpected expenses.

Common operating expenses

  • Landscaping and snow removal
  • Utilities for shared spaces
  • Routine maintenance and minor repairs
  • Insurance and management fees
  • Accounting, legal, and administrative costs

What boards should watch

  • Actual spending compared with the budget
  • Unusual or recurring variances
  • Assessment collections and outstanding balances
  • Unpaid invoices and upcoming obligations
  • Whether current assessments cover expected needs

Reserve funds: preparing for major assets

Reserve funds are intended for significant, infrequent projects that are too large for an ordinary operating budget. Examples may include roof replacement, parking-lot work, exterior painting, elevator modernization, or major mechanical-system repairs.

Funding these projects over time can reduce the likelihood of a sudden special assessment. It also helps the board plan around the useful life of the community’s shared assets instead of waiting for an emergency.

The role of a reserve study

A reserve study evaluates the association’s major components, estimates their remaining useful lives and replacement costs, and recommends a funding path. It gives the board a planning tool rather than a guarantee. Costs, inflation, construction conditions, and project timing can change, so the study should be reviewed and updated as appropriate.

The most useful reserve plan connects the physical condition of the property to the financial plan. Boards can then explain not only how much is being saved, but why the funding level is reasonable for the assets the community is responsible for maintaining.

How the two funds work together

Operating and reserve funds should be managed as parts of one financial picture. Operating money should not routinely be used to postpone long-term reserve planning, and reserve money should not be treated as a general-purpose account for daily shortfalls. Any transfer or exception should be evaluated against governing documents, applicable law, and professional guidance.
