Why ADHD Is Often Missed in Women: Underdiagnosis, Masking, and Late Recognition

Research is changing how clinicians understand ADHD in women and girls. A diagnosis may come later not because symptoms are new, but because they were less visible, compensated for, or mistaken for something else.

The short answer: ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition that can affect women throughout life, but it has historically been easier to recognize when symptoms are disruptive or externally visible. Inattentive symptoms, internalized distress, masking, and strong coping systems can delay recognition. When adult responsibilities increase or those coping systems stop working, ADHD may seem to have appeared suddenly even when earlier signs were present.

Women are increasingly seeking evaluations for ADHD after years of feeling overwhelmed, disorganized, forgetful, emotionally exhausted, or unable to keep up with demands that seem manageable for other people. Some describe a turning point after college, parenthood, a promotion, a relationship change, or the menopausal transition. Others begin wondering about ADHD after a child, partner, or friend receives a diagnosis.

These experiences do not prove that a person has ADHD. They do, however, reflect an important shift in the research: clinicians and researchers are paying more attention to how ADHD may present in women, how symptoms can be hidden by compensation, and why a diagnosis may be delayed for years.

Why ADHD may be missed in girls and women

ADHD does not have one universal appearance. Some people are primarily inattentive. Others have more hyperactive or impulsive symptoms, and many have a combination. Research on girls has found that inattentive patterns such as daydreaming, forgetfulness, disorganization, and difficulty sustaining effort may be less disruptive in a classroom than obvious hyperactivity or impulsive behavior. That can reduce the chance of a referral for evaluation. [1]

Social expectations can add another layer. Girls and women may be expected to be organized, emotionally responsive, prepared, and attentive to other people’s needs. When ADHD makes those tasks harder, some people respond by developing elaborate systems to compensate. They may overprepare, work late, avoid asking for help, rely on urgency, or spend significant energy trying to appear organized.

Masking is not a formal ADHD diagnosis, and not every woman with ADHD masks symptoms. In this context, the term describes efforts to hide, compensate for, or work around difficulties. Those efforts may help someone perform well in school or work while creating a large private cost in stress, exhaustion, shame, or burnout.

Late diagnosis does not necessarily mean late onset

ADHD is generally understood as a neurodevelopmental condition. Symptoms begin in childhood, although they may change over time and may become more noticeable as a person’s responsibilities increase. A person can be diagnosed for the first time as an adult even when the underlying pattern began much earlier. [5]

This is why an adult evaluation often includes questions about childhood behavior, school reports, family observations, friendships, organization, forgetfulness, and the ability to complete tasks. The goal is not to prove that a person was disruptive as a child. It is to look for a consistent developmental history that fits the diagnostic criteria and helps distinguish ADHD from conditions that can look similar.

The phrase adult-onset ADHD is still debated. Some longitudinal studies have identified adults with new ADHD-like symptoms and no clear childhood history. Other researchers argue that these findings may reflect limitations in retrospective recall, missed or masked childhood symptoms, changing environments, or symptoms caused by another condition. A 2026 systematic review concluded that evidence for a distinct late-onset neurological subtype remains inconclusive and emphasized comprehensive differential diagnosis and collateral history. [6]

In practical terms, a person who notices attention problems for the first time in adulthood deserves a careful assessment rather than an automatic conclusion in either direction. The symptoms may reflect previously unrecognized ADHD, depression, anxiety, trauma, sleep problems, medication effects, substance use, hormonal changes, another medical condition, or more than one issue at the same time.

How life changes can make symptoms more visible

Many people rely on structure, external deadlines, family support, or routines to manage attention and organization. When those supports change, the underlying difficulties may become harder to compensate for. College can remove the structure of a school day. A new job can require independent planning. Parenthood can add constant interruptions and competing priorities. A promotion can increase the number of projects that must be managed at once.

This does not mean that a life transition causes ADHD. It may reveal the gap between a person’s demands and the coping systems that previously kept symptoms manageable. The same person may look highly capable from the outside while using far more time and energy than others to maintain that performance.

What research says about hormones and ADHD

Researchers are studying how hormonal fluctuations may interact with ADHD symptoms across the menstrual cycle, pregnancy, the postpartum period, perimenopause, and menopause. A 2025 systematic review concluded that hormonal phases may be associated with symptom changes, but the evidence base remains limited and more research is needed. [3]

Perimenopause is especially relevant because changes in sleep, mood, attention, and executive function can overlap with ADHD symptoms. A 2025 population-based cohort study found that women with ADHD reported more severe perimenopausal symptoms than women without ADHD. The study shows an association, not that ADHD causes perimenopause or that every midlife attention problem is ADHD. [4]

Hormonal changes may make existing ADHD symptoms feel more difficult, but clinicians still need to consider other explanations. Sleep disruption, anxiety, depression, thyroid conditions, medication changes, and the cognitive effects of menopause can all affect attention and memory. Hormones may be part of the clinical picture without being the whole explanation.

What a thoughtful adult ADHD evaluation includes

There is no single test that can confirm ADHD. A careful evaluation considers current symptoms, the settings in which they occur, functional impairment, developmental history, family history, medical history, sleep, substance use, mood, anxiety, trauma, and other conditions that may overlap with ADHD. [5]

For women who have learned to compensate, it can help to describe the effort behind the outcome. Finishing a project does not necessarily mean there was no impairment if it required an all-night deadline, repeated reminders, extreme anxiety, or support from another person. At the same time, a clinician should avoid treating every experience of stress or forgetfulness as evidence of ADHD.

Collateral information can be useful when it is available. Old report cards, family observations, school records, and examples from different stages of life may help clarify whether symptoms were present earlier. The evaluation should also make room for the patient’s own account of what has changed and what kinds of situations create the most difficulty.

What treatment and support may involve

ADHD care is individualized. Depending on the evaluation, treatment may include medication management, skills-based strategies, therapy or coaching from appropriate professionals, workplace or school accommodations, sleep support, and treatment for co-occurring conditions. A plan may change over time as responsibilities, health, and goals change.

Questions people often ask

Can a woman have ADHD if she did well in school?

Yes. Academic performance alone does not rule out ADHD. Some people compensate through high effort, strong memory in certain subjects, external structure, perfectionism, or support from others. An evaluation looks at symptoms and impairment across development and settings rather than grades alone.

Does ADHD begin during menopause?

ADHD is generally considered a neurodevelopmental condition with symptoms beginning earlier in life. Menopause or perimenopause may worsen attention, sleep, mood, or executive-function difficulties and may bring previously compensated symptoms into view. New symptoms still require a broad clinical evaluation.

What if I did not have obvious hyperactivity as a child?

ADHD can present primarily through inattention. Daydreaming, losing things, forgetfulness, disorganization, slow task completion, and difficulty managing time may be more relevant than visible physical hyperactivity. A clinician will consider the full developmental pattern.
